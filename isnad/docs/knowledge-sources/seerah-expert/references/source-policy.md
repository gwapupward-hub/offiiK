## 1. Evidence Hierarchy

Preserve the Islamic Teacher Core's hierarchy:

1. Qur'an with verified Arabic text and sound contextual interpretation.
2. Authentic Prophetic hadith.
3. Verified Companion testimony and practice.
4. Critically assessed early Seerah, Maghazi, Tabaqat, Dala'il, genealogy, and historical reports.
5. Recognized classical Sunni synthesis.
6. Reliable modern research that transparently uses primary sources.

This is an authority hierarchy, not a rule that later levels are useless. Much biographical detail survives only in historical genres; use it with evidence labels proportionate to its transmission.

## 2. Foundational Source Families

Use relevant primary works and reliable editions, including:

- Qur'an and canonical hadith collections;
- the Maghazi material of Musa ibn 'Uqbah and Ma'mar ibn Rashid;
- Ibn Ishaq as preserved by Ibn Hisham and through other transmitters;
- al-Waqidi only with explicit caution about his severe hadith-critical weakness;
- Ibn Sa'd's *al-Tabaqat al-Kubra* with report-by-report assessment;
- al-Tabari's *Tarikh* with chain and source criticism;
- Dala'il works of al-Bayhaqi and Abu Nu'aym;
- early Shamā'il and Khasa'is material;
- genealogy, correspondence, documents, poetry, and local history when independently assessed.

Later syntheses include works by Ibn 'Abd al-Barr, al-Dhahabi, Ibn Sayyid al-Nas, Ibn al-Qayyim, Ibn Kathir, Ibn Hajar, al-Salihi, and other recognized scholars. Use each within its genre and method.

## 3. Use of Major Works

- **Ibn Ishaq/Ibn Hisham:** Foundational and early, but not automatically authentic report by report. Note Ibn Hisham's editorial role and that Ibn Ishaq's material survives through recensions and citations.
- **Al-Waqidi:** Useful for locating details and later historical tradition, but do not make his unsupported reports decisive. Identify corroboration independently.
- **Ibn Sa'd:** Valuable source collection whose reports vary with his transmitters.
- **Al-Tabari:** A compiler who commonly preserves chains and variants; inclusion is not endorsement.
- **Dala'il literature:** Assess miracle reports individually; devotional genre does not waive authentication.
- **Later biographies:** Useful for synthesis and source leads. Trace controversial details back whenever possible.

Do not use an author's overall stature as a blanket grade for every report in the work.

## 4. Modern Studies

Prefer modern works that:

- cite primary Arabic sources precisely,
- disclose chronology and authentication choices,
- distinguish report from reconstruction,
- engage variant accounts,
- avoid sectarian polemics and sensationalism,
- and show competence in Arabic, hadith, and late antique or Arabian context relevant to the claim.

Use archaeological, epigraphic, manuscript, geographic, or non-Muslim contemporary evidence when relevant, but explain its scope and uncertainty. It can illuminate context; it does not independently settle revelation or hadith authentication.

Do not treat a popular biography, documentary, map, lecture, or academic theory as primary evidence.

## 5. Verification and Citation

- Verify Qur'anic wording and do not assert sabab al-nuzul from mere thematic similarity.
- Verify hadith through Hadith Sciences Expert or recognized critical sources.
- Trace a historical claim to its earliest accessible source and route.
- Check whether several later books repeat one underlying report rather than provide independent corroboration.
- Verify Arabic quotations before using quotation marks.
- Identify where translations expand, paraphrase, or combine accounts.
- Distinguish explicit date, calculated date, conventional date, and approximation.
- Cite the source title and relevant section, report, or verified numbering. Never invent a page.
- Report a negative finding narrowly: “I did not locate this wording in the sources searched.”

## 6. Prohibited Sourcing

Do not rely decisively on:

- unsourced lectures, clips, quote cards, or children's retellings;
- polemical apologetic or anti-Islamic websites;
- search snippets or AI-generated citations;
- anonymous translations;
- dramatic dialogue or internal motives supplied only by a modern narrator;
- circular repetitions across derivative biographies;
- or fabricated poems, letters, treaties, relic claims, and predictions.

Use indexes and searchable databases for discovery, then verify the underlying text.
